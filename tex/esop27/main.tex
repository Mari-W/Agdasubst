\begin{abstract}
In dependently typed proof
assistants, intrinsically typed syntax promises elegant mechanizations: well-typed
terms are represented by well-typed data, substitution preserves
typing by construction, and type safety proofs collapse to
straightforward induction. For simple calculi such as the simply-typed
lambda calculus this promise is fulfilled. For realistic polymorphic systems, however,
the technique can become impractical: renamings and substitutions
indexed by other substitutions force proofs to transport terms across
equal types that are not definitionally equal. This
phenomenon---informally known as transfer hell---causes even routine
metatheoretic arguments to be dominated by administrative reasoning.  

This fresh perspective shows how to make intrinsically typed syntax
work in practice for polymorphic calculi. We show that giving substitutions canonical
computational behavior (via Agda rewriting) yields a
mechanization of System~F's metatheory in Agda that avoids
explicit transport reasoning.
 
Our approach simplifies proofs of metatheoretic
properties: typing preservation becomes definitional, substitution lemmas and corresponding
transfers disappear. Generally, when intrinsic encodings generate transports, the right move
is often  to strengthen definitional equality rather than add more lemmas. We extract practical
guidelines for applying this methodology. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Intrinsically typed syntax is widely recommended for mechanizing type
systems in dependently typed proof assistants. By representing
well-typed terms as well-typed data, typing invariants hold by
construction as in a Church-style presentation, so typing
preservation is immediate. For small 
calculi this approach is highly effective. However, for realistic
polymorphic calculi such as System~F, mechanizations often become
complicated: conceptually simple proofs become cluttered with
transports due to administrative equalities.

The simply-typed lambda calculus is the canonical example where
intrinsically typed syntax is particularly effective. \Cref{fig:simply-typed-syntax}
shows typical Agda definitions of the 
syntax of types, type contexts, variables, and expressions, which are
indexed by contexts and types (left column). Building on these definitions we
can define a small-step operational semantics and
prove type safety. This is because subject reduction holds by construction and progress
follows by a simple inductive proof (right column). We elide the definition  of the
substitution operator \AgdaFunction{$\_[\_]$}, as we discuss substitutions in detail
in \cref{sec:types}.

The intrinsic encoding makes type safety proofs so concise that they can serve as textbook
examples \cite{DBLP:journals/scp/KokkeSW20}. Nothing comparable to
transport reasoning arises in this development as only definitional
properties of substitutions are required. 
However, the same approach breaks down for polymorphic calculi and the remainder of this
paper explains how to recover some of its simplicity.

% TODO[PT]: define transfer hell precisely and what propositional vs definitional means
Transfer hell is the colloquial term for being forced to apply
type-changing lemmas to terms that are already known to be equal.
This phenomenon appears, for example,
in intrinsic mechanizations of System F: expression substitutions 
depend on type substitutions and type substitutions depend on
renamings; composing them yields types that are equal only
propositionally, forcing transports throughout proofs. 
The root problem is that substitutions that are propositionally equal
are not definitionally equal, so terms must repeatedly be transported
across equal types. 

One workaround is to state definitions and
proofs with explicit equivalence relations, but this practice is
cumbersome, inefficient, and far away from the concision of
definitional equality.
Our proposal thus shifts the focus to computation on substitutions: if extensionally equal
substitutions do not normalize identically, transport reasoning is unavoidable.

Hence, the simple message of this fresh perspective is: when intrinsically typed syntax
becomes complicated, the problem is often not the encoding of terms
but the definition of equality on substitutions. Strengthening
definitional equality can simplify metatheoretical proofs more effectively than
adding automation or additional lemmas.

We substantiate our claims with a case study on renaming and substitution in an
intrinsic encoding of System~F.
We strengthen definitional equality by internalizing the equational theory of substitutions
(the $\sigma$-calculus)
using Agda’s rewriting mechanism. As a
consequence, extensionally equal substitutions normalize to the same
form and transports disappear from routine proofs.

\subsection*{Related Work and Positioning}

To our knowledge, previous intrinsically typed mechanizations of
System~F 
\cite{10.1007/978-3-030-33636-3_10,10.1145/3678000.3678201,DBLP:journals/jar/BentonHKM12}
follow a natural design: expression syntax is indexed by typing
derivations and substitutions are indexed by substitutions at the type
level. While elegant at the level of definitions, this design makes
proofs brittle, because they are prone to transfer hell. 
% Because expression substitutions depend on type
% substitutions, and type substitutions depend on renamings, which again
% depend on type renamings, routine equalities require repeated
% transport across equal types, leading directly to transfer hell.
Our work eliminates transfers resulting from substitutions, thus avoiding transfer hell.

Recent work studies rewrite rules in dependent type theories,
including Agda~\cite{cockx:LIPIcs.TYPES.2019.2} and
Rocq~\cite{leray_et_al:LIPIcs.ITP.2024.26,10.1145/3776704}.
In Rewriting Type Theory (RTT), the reduction relation is extended by turning
propositional equalities into reduction rules: a term matched by the
left-hand side of an equation reduces to the right-hand side. 
Rewrites are safe if they preserve subject reduction and
consistency. The former is guaranteed by confluence of the combined
rewrite system, the latter by only rewriting
proved propositional equalities.
Our work is based on Agda's implementation of RTT.

Our approach is inspired by the work on Autosubst
\cite{schafer2015autosubst,Stark:2020:Mechanising,10.1145/3293880.3294101}.
While they rely on tactics to normalize substitutions, we do so by rewriting
\cite{10.1145/3434341}. We build on a 
standard functional representation of renamings and substitutions
\cite{DBLP:journals/jar/BentonHKM12}. 
The main challenge in setting up the $\sigma$-calculus is to fine-tune the
reduction behavior of the definitions with the rewrite rules to ensure that the combined
reduction relation remains terminating and confluent.

The syntax of System~F types uses the standard de Bruijn encoding of
variables by numbers. The type
$\AgdaDatatype{Type}~n$  (\cref{fig:system-f-types}) stands for the 
set of types with $n$ type variables in scope. Henceforth, we call
such $n$  a \emph{scope}.
Type variables for scope $n$ are drawn from the type $\AgdaDatatype{Var}\ n$, i.e.,
the set $\{0,\dots, n-1\}$, written as
\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero},
$\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}~\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}$,
$\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}~(\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}~\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero})$,
\dots and ranged over by {$\alpha$}.
The  \AgdaInductiveConstructor{$\forall\alpha$}-binder introduces a new type
variable, and \AgdaInductiveConstructor{$\Rightarrow$} is the infix
function type constructor. We call this style of syntax \emph{intrinsically scoped} as it
can only express well-scoped types. We let \AgdaFunction{T} range over types.

\Cref{tab:system-f-operator-inventory} summarizes the operator layer used throughout the development.
The table is intended as a reference map: renaming operators $\zeta :
\AgdaFunction{Ren}~n_1~n_2$ capture structure-preserving variable 
reindexing from scope $n_1$ to scope $n_2$, while substitution operators $\eta :

Two design choices are important for what follows.  First, renamings and substitutions are
represented functionally, which keeps composition and lifting explicit.  Second, the
operators in this interface are the symbols on which we later impose the $\sigma$-calculus
equations as rewrite rules.  This separation between interface and equations enables
us to control reduction behavior precisely.

This section presents the equalities of the $\sigma$-calculus. These are the equalities that
we \emph{actually} use for computing with renamings and substitutions, rather than their
defining equations 
in \cref{fig:system-f-ren-sub}. The \AgdaKeyword{opaque} blocks control this behavior.
Function symbols defined in an
\AgdaKeyword{opaque} block are usable outside the block, but they do
not reduce. The only reducing definitions in the renaming
block are \AgdaFunction{$\zeta~ \uparrow^R$} for lifting a renaming and
application of a renaming to a type. In the substitution block, only
the application of a substitution to a type reduces.

This selective reduction behavior is
essential for our approach because we want to compute using the laws
of the $\sigma$-calculus with first-class renamings  on top of the
thus defined symbols, rather than their definitions. Blocking their
reduction is necessary because the equalities from $\sigma$-calculus 
interfere adversely with the standard definitional equalities.
The original defining equalities are available for proofs: 
We can open the opaque definitions locally and proceed as usual.


Next we introduce equational laws of the
$\sigma$-calculus with first-class renamings \cite{schafer2015autosubst}.
All of these equalities are supported by proofs using the definitions in
\cref{fig:system-f-ren-sub}. The proofs are available in the
supplement, but they are not included here for space reasons.

The first group presents computation rules for substitutions.
\SFTypeSubstitutionBeta
Starting with the application of a substitution to a variable, there is one
equation for each possible form of a substitution: extended
substitution, lifted renaming, and composition of substitutions. The
final equation in this group characterizes lifting in terms of extension,
weakening, and composition. It is \emph{not} possible to use this
equation to define lifting in \cref{fig:system-f-ren-sub} as it would
lead to circularity.

The second group contains laws about interactions between substitutions.
\SFTypeSubstitutionInteraction
Composition is associative and it distributes over extension. First weakening and then
extending with $T$ has no effect, as $T$ is substituted for a non-existing variable. The
identity substitution is a right and left identity. We conclude this group with two 
$\eta$-laws about the interaction of extension and
weakening.\footnote{In the statement of \AgdaFunction{$\eta$-id}, we
  write $\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}~ \{n\}$ to tell Agda's type
  checker that $n$ is the scope of type variable
  \AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}. This notation supplies an
  \emph{implicit argument} that Agda can usually infer.}

There are analogous rules for renamings corresponding to the first two
groups. These rules are omitted for space reasons. 

The third group presents laws that govern the composition of different
kinds of term traversal, that is, the behavior of the identity
renaming and the four possible compositions of renamings and
substitutions.
\SFTypeMonad

The final group contains laws that mediate
between substitutions and renamings. Substitution of a
lifted renaming is a renaming; the composition of two
lifted renamings is the lifting of their composition as renamings.
\SFTypeCoincidence
In general, the extraction of renamings from
substitutions requires a dedicated solving
strategy~\cite{10.1145/3293880.3294101} and we have omitted some
further coincidence laws for brevity.

Orienting these equations from left to right results in a confluent
rewrite system for the $\sigma$-calculus that we install in Agda's computation engine using the
\AgdaKeyword{REWRITE} pragma \cite{10.1145/3434341}. Confluence of
these rewrite equations is mechanically checked by Agda using
the flags \texttt{--rewriting --confluence-check}. We discuss safety considerations for
rewriting in \cref{sec:safety}. From now on, all 
equations shown in this section \cref{sec:laws-renam-subst-1} are
treated as definitional equalities.

Firstly, our development already saves one

\ExportProofTerm

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Unfold the definition of expression substitution at the inner
        application of $\sigma_1$, exposing the \AgdaFunction{subst}

  \item Remove the \AgdaFunction{subst} that sits inside the term under

  \item Reveal yet another \AgdaFunction{subst} introduced by the substitution application of $\sigma_2$

\subsection{Safety Conditions for Rewriting}
\label{sec:safety}

Rewrite rules and the reductions of a dependently typed
system interact in non-trivial
ways~\cite{cockx:LIPIcs.TYPES.2019.2,leray_et_al:LIPIcs.ITP.2024.26,10.1145/3776704}.
According to RTT's criteria, we can make the equations from \cref{sec:laws-renam-subst-1}
definitional because they preserve subject reduction and consistency. 

To uphold subject reduction, the critical property of the newly added rewriting
equations is that their \emph{union with the existing reduction
  relation} is confluent. Agda already provides a standard set of confluent 
reduction rules: $\beta$, $\eta$ (function and record expansion),
$\delta$ (definition unfolding), and $\iota$ (dependent pattern matching clauses).  
Agda checks confluence of the extended system using a
conservative set of simple syntactic criteria~\cite{10.1145/3434341}.

%% PT: not our business in this paper
% This check does not require termination. Notably, termination is \emph{not}
% a critical property, because it does not affect soundness or consistency, 
% it is only observable through never-terminating type checking
% but does no further harm.

Some $\beta$ and $\iota$ reductions arising from the functional
definitions of renamings and substitutions in \cref{fig:system-f-ren-sub}
interfere with the intended rewrite equalities so that confluence breaks. For this reason, we
explicitly hide these definitions inside an \AgdaKeyword{opaque} block,
so that only the equations we install with \AgdaKeyword{REWRITE} define
their reduction behaviour.

Without the opaque definitions, it may happen that the left-hand side of an equation reduces
to a normal form, which is different from the right-hand side. Thus, confluence would be
violated. 
As a concrete example, consider the equation
\AgdaFunction{$\eta$-law} (left) in connection with the definition of composition (right):
\begin{align*}

  &
\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaBound{η₁}\,\AgdaFunction{⨟ˢ}\,\AgdaBound{η₂}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\AgdaBound{α}\,&\AgdaSymbol{=}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaBound{η₁}\,\AgdaBound{α}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\AgdaFunction{[}\,\AgdaBound{η₂}\,\AgdaFunction{]ˢ}    
\end{align*}
Without the \AgdaKeyword{opaque} block, the definition of composition
would $\delta$-unfold the inner application
\AgdaFunction{⟨}\,\AgdaFunction{wkᴿ}\,\AgdaFunction{⟩}\,\AgdaFunction{⨟ˢ}\,\AgdaBound{η}
to
\AgdaSymbol{$\lambda$}\,\AgdaBound{α}\,\AgdaSymbol{$\to$}\,\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}\,\AgdaBound{α}\AgdaSymbol{)}
so that the left-hand side reduces as follows:
\begin{align*}

\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}\,\AgdaFunction{\&ˢ}\,\AgdaBound{η}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\AgdaFunction{∙ˢ}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaSymbol{$\lambda$}\,\AgdaBound{α}\,\AgdaSymbol{$\to$}\,\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}\,\AgdaBound{α}\AgdaSymbol{)}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\\
&\AgdaDatatype{≡}\,
\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\AgdaFunction{∙ˢ}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaSymbol{$\lambda$}\,\AgdaBound{α}\,\AgdaSymbol{$\to$}\,\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}\,\AgdaBound{α}\AgdaSymbol{)}\AgdaSymbol{)}
\end{align*}

% so that the entire left-hand side reduces to
% \AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaInductiveConstructor{zero}\AgdaSymbol{)}\,\AgdaFunction{∙ˢ}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaSymbol{$\lambda$}\,\AgdaBound{α}\,\AgdaSymbol{$\to$}\,\AgdaBound{η}\,\AgdaSymbol{(}\AgdaInductiveConstructor{suc}\,\AgdaBound{α}\AgdaSymbol{)}\AgdaSymbol{)}.
Two issues arise. First, the definitional reduction happens \emph{before} the rewrite engine
tries to match the left-hand side of \AgdaFunction{$\eta$-law} syntactically. Thus the

Second, if the rewrite rule were admitted, confluence would fail. The two

\subsection{Equational Theory for Expression Substitution}
\label{sec:equat-theory-expr}
With functoriality for expression substitution in place, one might ask
whether we can go further and install a full equational theory for
expression substitution as definitional equalities, just as we did at
the type level. Indeed, the $\sigma$-calculus for type-substitution-indexed
expression substitutions is an extension of the one for
type substitutions in \cref{sec:laws-renam-subst-1}, and the
same holds analogously for expression renamings.

Expression substitutions carry additional operators that have no
counterpart at the type level.
Every law involving extension or weakening splits into two
expression-level laws.  One governs the expression variants
(\AgdaFunction{\_∙ˢ\_}, \AgdaFunction{wkˢ}) and directly mirrors the
type-level rule \cref{sec:laws-renam-subst-1}. The other governs the type variants
(\AgdaFunction{\_∙ˢ*\_}, \AgdaFunction{wkˢ*}) and is genuinely
new. We illustrate this pattern with the $\eta$-id law. The expression
variant
\SFExprSubEtaIdSub
is a direct transcription of \AgdaFunction{$\eta$-id} from
\cref{sec:laws-renam-subst-1}, whereas the type variant
\SFExprSubEtaIdSubStar
asserts that extending the type-binding weakening with the topmost
type variable \AgdaInductiveConstructor{`\,zero} yields the identity
expression substitution. The same doubling applies to the remaining
laws in which extension or weakening appears ($\eta$, interact, distributivity and some $\beta$ laws).

The full equational system is included in the supplement, and Agda
verifies its confluence.
For the confluence check to go through, we had to artificially block
reduction of the traversal functions \AgdaFunction{\_∣\_[\_]ᴿ}
and \AgdaFunction{\_∣\_[\_]ˢ} and re-install each of their
defining clauses as a rewrite rule. In the type application case, rewriting must perform two
reduction steps at once to satisfy the triangle
criterion. One reduction normalizes the type
index; the other pushes substitution into the expression.

\subsection{Practical Guidelines for Other Intrinsic Developments}
\label{sec:tower}

We have now made the equations of the $\sigma$-calculus definitional
at two levels of the syntactic hierarchy: at the type level and at
the expression level, where the latter depends on the former.
In general, this strategy applies to
arbitrarily tall towers of intrinsically typed syntaxes, in
which each level's type indices themselves support substitution.

More precisely, consider a tower $T_0, T_1, \dots, T_n$ of syntactic
sorts in which the variables and substitutions of $T_{i+1}$ are
indexed by the contexts and substitutions of $T_0, \dots, T_i$. The
recipe proceeds bottom-up:
\begin{quote}
\noindent\fbox{%
\begin{minipage}{0.96\linewidth}
\textbf{Methodology Recipe.}
To avoid transfer hell in intrinsically typed towers:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Start at the base sort $T_0$: define renamings/substitutions and install
        the $\sigma$-calculus equations as rewrite rules.
  \item For each higher sort $T_i$, define syntax and substitution
        \emph{on top of} already-definitional lower-level substitutions.
  \item Prove the $\sigma$-calculus laws for $T_i$ and install them as rewrite rules.
  \item Check confluence of the combined reduction relation after each level.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{quote}
% \begin{enumerate}
%   \item Define $T_0$ together with its renamings and substitutions,
%         and install the $\sigma$-calculus equations for $T_0$ as
%         rewrite rules.
%   \item For each $i$ from $1$ to $n$, define $T_i$ \emph{on top of}
%         the already-definitional substitution machinery of
%         $T_0, \dots, T_{i-1}$, prove the corresponding
%         $\sigma$-calculus laws for $T_i$, and install them as
%         rewrite rules.
% \end{enumerate}
The key is that the laws for all levels $<i$ are already definitional,
when we define and prove the laws for level $i$.
Otherwise, the types of the level-$i$ operations would no longer
match definitionally, and we would be back in transfer hell. 

The size of the equational theory grows with each level. A
substitution at level $i$ can be lifted under a binder of any sort
$T_0, \dots, T_{i-1}$, and each kind of binder induces its own
family of laws, for weakening and extension, and the
corresponding interactions with composition and traversal. At the
type level this contributes no additional laws. At the expression
level we already saw a few new laws, namely the laws governing
weakening and extension by types. At level three one would add
even more laws, and so on. The rule count therefore grows
linearly with the level, but each new family follows the same
template as the previous one, so the proof effort per family stays
constant.

As an example from the literature, the language System
$\mathrm{F}^{\uparrow}_{C}$~\cite{10.1145/2103786.2103795}
features three levels: kinds, types, and
expressions. Each level carries its own variables
and substitution. Using our approach, it should be possible to construct
an intrinsically typed representation for it and establish the
substitution theory for all three levels uniformly, without any transfer
reasoning.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Intrinsically typed syntax facilitates elegant mechanizations by making
well-typed programs representable only as well-typed data. For
polymorphic calculi this promise is often undermined by pervasive
transport reasoning. Our experience with System~F
shows that the difficulty does not stem from binding structure or
indexing itself, but from a mismatch between substitution and
definitional equality: substitutions that are extensionally equal fail
to compute to a canonical form. By internalizing the equational laws of
the $\sigma$-calculus as definitional equalities, substitutions become
canonical and routine transports disappear from proofs. The resulting
developments recover the elegance and concision seen in the simply-typed case while
scaling to towers of syntactic sorts. In our System~F case study, we observe a decrease of
code size by a factor of 3--4 and a speedup by a factor of 2.
More broadly, this fresh perspective proposes a practical recipe
for mechanizing type systems in dependently typed proof assistants:
when intrinsically typed encodings become proof-heavy, a promising
strategy is often to strengthen definitional equality rather than
introduce additional lemmas or automation.
